abstract: "Ependymomas (EPN) are brain tumors, arising in the central nervous system in both children and adults. Supratentorial ependymomas (ST-EPN) are predominantly driven by YAP1- and ZFTA-fusion proteins, which are linked to distinct molecular subtypes and clinical outcomes.To study tumorigenesis of these ST-EPN types in a human background in vitro, we generated and molecularly characterized YAP1- and ZFTA-fusion driven models using cerebral brain organoids derived from human embryonic stem cells.Single cell transcriptome analyses of cerebral brain organoids developing over time demonstrated a high abundance of radial glia (RG)-like progenitor cells, the proposed cellular origin of EPN, in eleven days old organoids. In older organoids, the abundance of these cells went down as they matured into more differentiated cell types. Organoids electroporated at day 11 with either YAP1- or ZFTA-fusion expressing plasmids developed ectopic masses mimicking histological and molecular features of human ST-EPN tumors. Both YAP1- and ZFTA-fusion positive organoids were tumorigenic when engrafted into the mouse brain. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses of the organoids demonstrated that cells overexpressing the YAP1-fusion show a skewed differentiation towards RG lineages, while overexpression of the ZFTA-fusion results in a more neuronal differentiation trajectory, consistent with observations in patient-derived tumors. Moreover, we identified interactions between tumor cells and non-neoplastic developing brain cells, which may promote tumorigenesis and could potentially be targeted therapeutically.Our findings demonstrate that cerebral brain organoids can be used to study the tumorigenesis and biology of supratentorial EPN in vitro in a human context of a non-malignant environment.Supratentorial ependymoma are brain tumors in both children and adults, which are characterized by gene fusions containing YAP1 or ZFTA. Currently, there are no human cell models to mimic tumor formation and progression in the lab. To generate such a laboratory model, the fusion genes were introduced into lab-grown “mini-brains” (brain organoids) to model ependymoma formation. form. The resulting tumor-like growths closely resembled real human tumors. The t2 fusions affected cell development differently, matching patterns seen in patients. Hence, these models are a promising tool to study tumor formation within the developmental context."
